A working draft

Take a look, add your feedback, and come ready to workshop these principles and rituals in our next session.

5.1 Our Operating Principles

Operating principles aren’t just abstract ideals — they’re about how we make decisions. These four pillars outline our shared framework for how we collaborate, protect our focus, and drive meaningful results.

High trust

Autonomy over oversight. We view each other as capable and well-intentioned. Our trust means eliminating micromanagement and giving everyone the space to own their execution.

High agency

Initiative over instruction. We value proactivity over passivity. Having agency means stepping into the driver’s seat and owning the entire execution from start to finish — celebrating the wins and embracing the learning experiences.

High independence

Asynchronous over assemblies. Our workflow is asynchronous and independent by design. We preserve our focus, meeting only when necessary to move work forward.

Mandate and space to experiment

Exploration over caution. We embrace exploration to fuel innovation, creating an environment where mistakes are moments for growth. Provided we share the lessons, an unsuccessful experiment is a valuable data point, not a failure.

5.2 Rituals and Cadences

At the engagement level

Every engagement moves through three stages:

Pre-flight

We align the team on the problem, and on how we will work together.

Mid-flight

We share observations on what is working or needs to change — before it becomes a problem.

Post-flight

We share reflections on learnings, findings, and opportunities for improvement.

Weekly check-ins

We hold a full-team huddle once a week to collaborate, support one another, and keep everyone connected to the broader vision. This is not a routine status update.

Frequency

Weekly.

Length

Under 45 minutes.

Format

Open sharing — whatever is on your mind, whether blockers, gratitude, or small wins.

Design critiques

We strive to work, grow, and improve together. Critique is one of the most generative practices a design team can do, if done well. The goal is to collect initial reactions and observations — to understand how our work resonates and where it can be strengthened. This is not a place for prescriptive feedback. Our process draws on Pixar’s “Braintrust”, the candid peer-review approach popularised by Ed Catmull in Creativity, Inc.

Setting the tone

The facilitator keeps the conversation in the right register throughout the session.

Setting the context

The presenter anchors the room with background — the goals and the current state of the design.

Setting the perspective

The critic offers initial reactions and emotional observations. The focus is on impact, not instructions: “this part feels confusing”, not “move this button”.

Suggestions should be offered only if the presenter specifically asks for them.

Cross-project conversations

To keep our work sharp, every designer takes part in regular cross-project check-ins. When you’re deep inside a project, it’s easy to miss the blind spots — unconscious assumptions, subtle scope drift, and missed opportunities.

Stepping outside

Speak with a designer outside of your project. A fresh pair of eyes will spot what has become invisible from the inside, like hidden assumptions.

Keeping it informal

Stay open and casual. Have these conversations regularly — over a coffee break or as a quick huddle. The idea is a perspective-driven sounding board, not a formal review.

Rotating the lens

Seek different perspectives. There’s no rule that you have to stick with the same partner each time — in fact, we encourage inviting varied voices to build richer feedback loops.

Onboarding

A new team member’s first two weeks will always feel overwhelming, and that’s completely okay — it’s not a problem to fix, just an event to prepare for. For us, onboarding is a collective team responsibility, not just an HR checklist. Here’s what a typical onboarding could look like:

Before day one

Make them feel welcome with a message, and line up an onboarding buddy who has the bandwidth to make time and conversation.

First two weeks

Give them space to observe and ask questions before assigning them to deliver. Introduce them broadly — across disciplines and seniority, not just their immediate work.

Early on

Give them an anchor project — a small piece of work that lets them apply existing skills and build context.

Around week four

Sit down together and set goals to begin building a growth plan.

After each onboarding

Collect feedback and improve for the next one.

5.3 How We Show Up

Working in government means shaping lives within complex systems. Our decisions are rarely black and white; they require balancing competing priorities. In this environment, how we work matters as much as what we achieve. The following commitments serve as our compass, ensuring our craft always serves the public good.

Honesty over courtesy

Don’t play it safe; give honest feedback. High-quality work requires real transparency. That means coming to rituals fully prepared and engaged, ready to discuss what’s hard — not just what’s succeeding.

Collective over individual

Look beyond your own deliverables. We build better together than we ever could alone. That means sharing knowledge freely before anyone has to ask, and measuring impact by how the whole team crosses the finish line.

Collaboration over territory

Lean into other disciplines. The lines between design, product, and engineering are shifting, and embracing that overlap leads to stronger outcomes than hiding behind a narrow job description. That means learning how other specialties think, and engaging where our roles intersect.

Experimentation over status quo

Stay open to evolving. A well-documented experiment upskills the whole team, not just the person who ran it. That means moving past comfortable routines to test new tools or processes, and openly calling out what works and what fails.

Momentum over paralysis

Be comfortable with uncertainty. Moving forward in ambiguous spaces is how we uncover the answers we need. That means exercising high agency to make the call, and high independence in discerning when to pull others in.

Impact over delivery

Future-proof the work. The standards we set, the way we work, and the lessons we learn matter far beyond the immediate outcome. Share reflections openly, document methodologies, and treat every engagement as a chance to shape the culture of public-sector design.

Capability over shipping

Enable partners for the long term. Working with agency partners isn’t just about delivery; it’s about ensuring they can sustain the work after our exit. Slow down to explain the process and transfer knowledge, even when the engagement is moving quickly.

Resilience over resistance

Look at the big picture. Act with patience and respect while learning to work fluidly within complex government frameworks. Knowing how to navigate these systems keeps decisions grounded in real-world impact on the public we serve.

5.4 The Practice in Practice

We are actively exploring the possibilities of design in an AI-enabled environment. Below is a snapshot of how we operate today. As the technological landscape changes, our methods will too — and this page will grow along with us.

These insights were last updated in May 2026.

  • Designing and thinking with AI. We use Claude as a thinking partner for drafting, synthesising, sense-making, and stress-testing concepts.
  • Prototyping with real tools. We are moving beyond click-through mockups, experimenting with tools like Cursor to build functional prototypes that work with real data and real workflows.
  • Synthesising meetings with AI. We turn discussions into shared knowledge with tools like Granola, capturing and distilling conversations so insights don’t stay trapped in private notes.
  • Working in the open. Documentation, decisions, and drafts live in shared spaces — currently Notion and Craft — so the whole team can see, react, and build on what’s being created.
  • Understanding classification and sensitivity. We must know what counts as classified or sensitive data before passing it to any AI tool. Working in government means respecting clear security tiers — Official, Restricted, and Confidential — and this remains a permanent practice even as our workflows evolve.

The list above will likely look different every six months. Tools will evolve and new ones will emerge, but our mindset stays constant: stay curious, stay open to trying new things, and never stop asking if there’s a better way to do the work.
