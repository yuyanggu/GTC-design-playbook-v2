Foreword

For us, the GTC Design Team

This playbook is for us, the GovTech Consulting Design team. We focus on a specific part of design: discovery and creating the conditions for action. Not building, not developing. We work upstream, where the problem gets defined and the direction gets set. If you’re on the team, this is your document.

Open for a reason

Because good thinking gets sharper with honest reactions. We made it public so anyone can read it, push back on it, or bring it up over a meal. That kind of conversation is exactly what we’re hoping for.

Not a methods guide

You won’t find research techniques or workshop instructions here. What you will find is our point of view: on what design actually is and where it’s going, on why discovery is changing more dramatically than most people realise, on why prototyping means building to test and not building to release, on how the lines between design, product, and engineering are shifting, and on what it means to do all of this in government, for the public, at this particular moment in time.

Always in draft

This is not an excuse to leave things unfinished. It’s a declaration that we are in a moment of genuine flux, learning by doing, testing what we believe, and updating this as we go. Every project is a prototype. So is this.

2.1 Foundations that Guide Us

Government services exist to serve everyone. Whether we are designing for a resident accessing a public portal or the officer working the system from inside, our ultimate goal remains the same: ensuring a reliable, effective experience for whoever is on the other side of the screen.

In order to do our work well, we must operate within the guardrails of the public sector.

The system we work in:

  • Public frameworks — that govern what services or products can be built.
  • Governance structure — that shapes how policy decisions are made.
  • Accountability to the public — that serves as the ultimate purpose of our designs.

The breadth we design for:

Equitable access to public services

We design for everyone, including all abilities, age, language, and digital fluency.

A united government experience

We design for the integrity of the entire civic journey, connecting public services into a seamless experience that feels like one government, not many.

Public officers for improved services

We design to improve tools and processes for officers so they can deliver services with greater confidence.

2.2 Our Principles of Execution

Great public services are the result of well-informed and deliberate choices. In a landscape where technical capacity can outpace decision-making, we hold ourselves to the following principles to ensure our work maintains technical rigour and depth, whilst centring human experiences.

Our operating principles

  • Anchoring in discovery. Uncovering what is true at the start ensures we confidently solve the right problem from day one. While the depth of discovery scales to match the project, embedding this practice into our workflow remains non-negotiable. (See 2.3 and 4.2: Discovery.)
  • Designing for the whole system. Looking beyond the digital interface allows us to shape the entire delivery chain, including policy, process, operations, and public experience. A product only truly succeeds when the ecosystem supporting it thrives. (See 2.3: Through a Discovery Lens.)
  • Prototyping upstream. Bringing prototypes into early discovery generates real-world signals faster. Because the cost of building has collapsed, using prototypes early serves as our most effective tool for de-risking critical decisions before we scale.
  • Building partner capability. Equipping agency partners to own and iterate on the work ensures lasting impact long after we leave. True success means moving beyond temporary delivery to leave our partners entirely self-sufficient. (See 3.3: Building Lasting Capability.)
  • Unifying team effort. Collapsing the boundaries between team members and their specialised disciplines allows us to cross-examine ideas from multiple angles simultaneously. Working as a unified team during discovery lets us navigate new technologies seamlessly, replacing slow handoffs.

What we’re still working out

Where exactly these discipline boundaries should sit. There is no settled answer yet, and we aren’t waiting for one. We are intentionally experimenting our way toward it, and we expect what we learn to evolve how we design and build.

The human anchor in AI

AI has shifted the way we work — from the shape of our team, to the velocity of our tools, and the methods we use to solve problems. The GTC Design team has been moving alongside these changes to improve our work processes, including:

  • Reducing administrative cognitive load in our workflows.
  • Elevating how we collaborate with agency partners.

But AI outputs often miss the operational nuances that only exist in the physical world. Public services are deeply human ecosystems, and the human judgment required to navigate them can’t be resolved in a prompt. This judgment is what drives our most important decisions:

  • Mapping out how a new digital tool will change the rhythm of an officer’s daily routines.
  • Translating lived user experiences into long-term design strategies.
  • Turning competing institutional priorities into a roadmap that agencies can act on.

We rely on technology to handle the heavy lifting, but strategy, ethics, and human discernment cannot be replicated by a model. They remain at the core of our craft, and they are the reason our team shows up the way we do.

Public services are deeply human ecosystems, and the human judgment required to navigate them can’t be resolved in a prompt.

2.3 Through a Discovery Lens

No matter how complex the legacy structures, or how siloed the agencies may be, finding an engagement’s core problem begins with discovery. When we work with agency partners, our first goal is to build a shared understanding of the systems, constraints, and the people involved, while actively testing early assumptions. As the stated problem is rarely the root problem, racing ahead on assumptions is a fast track to solving a problem that may not exist.

The cost of bypassing discovery

Skipping the discovery phase fundamentally shifts the product’s risk, scope, and strategic focus. Here is what happens when an insight-led endeavour is replaced by unverified assumptions:

Without discovery (assumption-led)
With discovery (insight-led)

High risk: building unverified features that fail post-launch, draining agency time and budget.

Bloated scope: reacting to symptoms by trying to solve every adjacent issue at once.

Output-focused: delivering a rigid product that performs a function but misses the human context.

De-risked: validating concepts early to save effort, cost, and structural friction.

Strategic scope: focusing collective effort on features that drive the highest systemic impact.

Outcome-focused: delivering an adaptable capability that solves a real, verified user problem.

The GTC design path

Complex products and engagements tend to stall in one of two ways.

  • Building without evidence (the blind sprint): building straight into engineering on unverified assumptions, then discovering post-launch that the problem was misunderstood.
  • Discovering without building (the analysis paralysis): months of research that surfaces brilliant insights, but they stay trapped in slide decks and never become working software.

The GTC path runs directly through the middle. We treat discovery and rapid prototyping as one continuous loop, ensuring that every solution we build is grounded in real-world evidence, and every insight we uncover is immediately tested in a working prototype.

Approach A — build without discovery: solving the wrong problem with high confidence. Approach B — discovery without build: the problem is identified but nothing gets built. The GTC path runs between them — discovery + prototyping — producing real signal early through continuous experimentation upstream.

What discovery means to us: a two-phase approach

The landscape of discovery is changing as quickly as the technology around it, but our purpose remains unchanged: to find what’s true before building a solution. What has changed is our ability to now get from insight to evidence faster than ever.

Rather than treating discovery as a linear phase, the GTC Design team sees discovery as a loop that runs in two subphases which then feed each other: (1) Discover to Understand and (2) Discover to Validate.

Subphase 1: Discover to UnderstandTo anchor our strategy in facts rather than assumptions

This part largely hasn’t changed from traditional discovery processes. We continue to deeply analyse the current landscape, protecting project teams from the risk of rushing ahead to solve the wrong problems.

The shift
The value

Automating administrative overhead. AI is used to manage operational burdens like transcription and initial data calibration, maximising time and energy for designers to focus on high-value synthesis and root-cause analysis. (See 2.2: The human anchor in AI.)

Uncovering objective ground truth. Mapping systemic realities and stakeholder constraints helps us align agency partners around the root problem, so resources and effort are not wasted building solutions to the wrong problems.

Subphase 2: Discover to ValidateThe extension — to turn abstract insights into working proof

This subphase compresses the gap between strategy and execution. We pull prototyping out of late-stage development and into early discovery, so ideas meet reality before we commit real engineering effort.

The shift
The value

Deploying interactive concepts early. Instead of relying on static mock-ups and early user stories, designers use AI to build responsive prototypes at a fraction of traditional time and cost.

Validating direction with concrete evidence. By testing these prototypes against the data, we capture actual user behaviour before development begins. This catches operational friction early — saving officers from administrative roadblocks and delivering a reliable experience for the public. (See 4.2: Discovery — Subphase 2.)

2.4 Success in our eyes

The pace of development has accelerated dramatically. But speed applied to the wrong problem is just the wrong problem arrived at sooner. As public needs grow more complex, getting it right takes more than moving quickly; it requires the judgment to know when to push forward and when to slow down — especially within institutions where the default reaction is to settle for “how it’s always been done”.

Below are the indicators that show we are moving in the right direction:

  • The right problems were named. We articulated the foundational systemic bottlenecks, ensuring public energy was directed where it mattered most.
  • The hard line was held. We pushed back on assumptions and legacy framings rather than accepting them at face value, to protect the integrity of the final product.
  • The technology served the people. We empowered partner agencies to deploy user-centric solutions that accurately solved real friction points and elevated the citizen experience.
  • The agency’s capabilities were built. We established sustainable operational frameworks, enabling agency partners to drive the work forward without long-term dependency on GTC.
  • The standard was modelled. We demonstrated a level of rigour, empathy, and speed that set a new operational template for the agency’s future endeavours.

3.1 The Shape of Engagement

Partnership is how we work. Agencies bring the domain knowledge and the mandate; we bring discovery capability, technology, and product expertise. Together, we build something that creates real impact for the public. Below shows where our partnership begins and ends, and why both points matter.

Diagram — to come

A horizontal timeline: Inception → Discovery & Experimentation → Build & Launch. We enter at inception; we exit at one of two points — Exit A, handing over a validated roadmap and working prototype after experimentation; or Exit B, after the build is delivered and live.

Where we enter

The earlier we’re brought in to help shape the problem, the more value the work creates. If the direction is already locked, the scope fixed without discovery, or the agency already committed to a specific build, our ability to help agency partners solve core issues drops significantly. Wherever we enter, we tell agencies what’s still achievable from that point, so both sides begin with a shared, realistic picture of what’s possible.

Where we exit

We don’t leave teams hanging. Our intention is always to build up the agency’s own capability to carry the work forward, and that transfer of ownership starts on day one, not at the handover line. We typically step away at one of two natural exit points:

Exit Option A — After experimentation

We hand off after Discovery Subphase 2, leaving a validated, high-confidence roadmap and working prototype directly in the agency’s hands for their downstream teams to build. (See 4.2: Discovery.)

Exit Option B — After build and delivery

We stay through the actual build and launch, stepping away only when the service is live and the agency team is fully equipped to run it independently. In special cases that call for it, we will also stay through the support phase. (See 4.3: Building and Delivery; 4.4: Support and Maintenance.)

3.2 The Work We Build On

Types of engagement we take on

Our work starts with two conditions: (1) a problem worth solving, and (2) a partner ready to work alongside us to solve it. Before committing to an engagement, we work together to understand the engagement landscape — whether the budget is secured, what it covers, and whether it aligns with the proposed scope. Surfacing this early builds the transparency that makes for a productive partnership. (See 4.1: Qualifying.)

The scenario
What we do

Legacy system overhaul. An agency’s legacy system is failing both users and officers.

  • Discover what is actually broken.
  • Map the full service.
  • Prototype what an improved version could look like.

Digital policy validation. A ministry wants to understand whether a new policy can be delivered digitally.

  • Run discovery to validate the need.
  • Surface the constraints.
  • Experiment with what a service could be before any build commitment is made.

System & operational consolidation. An agency is streamlining multiple fragmented systems into a single platform, requiring entirely new internal workflows.

  • Discover to rethink the people, process, and workflows around it.
  • Rebuild the operation alongside the new platform.

What we don’t do

We work as an advisory partner, not as a permanent vendor or an ongoing operational arm. Our goal is to build capabilities that outlast us (more on that in 3.3: Building Lasting Capability). To protect that focus, here’s a quick look at what’s in and out of scope:

What we don’t do
What we do instead

Take over the entire engagement cycle. We are a consulting and discovery team, not the permanent design or engineering function for an agency.

Accelerate, enable, and pilot solutions. We focus on the discovery arc, building proofs of concept and value (POC/POV) before handing over to our agency partner’s engineering team.

Write funding papers or investment proposals. This includes IV papers, procurement documents, and the like.

Inform strategic thinking. We provide the foundational insights and data that help an agency shape and back up their internal case.

Design without being involved in the discovery. If a solution has already been decided and we’re asked only to make it look good, that isn’t an engagement we can add real value to.

Design based on discovery insights. We ensure our design is deeply connected to user research, constraints, and verified needs. (See 2.3: Through a Discovery Lens.)

Run isolated research tasks without a discovery arc. We are not a research-for-hire team delivering standalone tasks; research has to connect to discovery, hypothesis, and action.

Connect research to clear next steps. We ensure every research piece connects to a full discovery arc with clear hypothesis, validated findings, and tangible action. (See 2.3 and 4.2.)

Deliver a prototype and walk away. We don’t leave partners without roadmaps or context in the handoff.

Build capability for the next step forward. We guide partners to solve the right problems and set up an environment to maintain the work after we leave. (See 3.3: Building Lasting Capability.)

A note on research-focused engagements

We help agency partners solve core challenges through a complete discovery arc. However, we do make exceptions for specific institutional requirements — a longer, research-led runway before the process begins, or a partial discovery phase without ideation (see 2.3: Through a Discovery Lens). Even in these rare cases, the work must still connect to a larger arc of hypothesis and action.

3.3 Building Lasting Capability

We measure our success not by the solutions we leave behind, but by the people who carry them forward. Ownership sits with our agency partners; they hold deep domain knowledge and the long-term stakes in the engagement’s success. Beyond helping agencies fill immediate skill gaps early on, our role is to cultivate the structures, tools, and talent necessary for this work to survive — and keep improving — long after we are gone.

Capacity only grows under the right conditions. For the work to sustain itself, three things need to come from the agency side:

  • A clearly identified business owner — someone accountable for the outcome.
  • A sponsor with decision-making authority — someone who can clear the path when it matters.
  • A shared sense of what success looks like — agreed between us, and early.

Risks

Engagements without a clearly identified business owner stall. When no one on the agency side has the authority to make decisions or the mandate to drive the work, even the best discovery and prototyping struggles to land.

Two tracks of capability building

There are two agency teams that we help grow, and they run in parallel alongside the engagement:

  • The product team — the people who design, build, and iterate on the service.
  • The operations team — the people who run and sustain it once it’s live.
Diagram — to come

Capability building shown as a parallel track that starts on day one and runs the full length of the engagement, with two sub-tracks inside it: the product team and the operations team.

The product teamThe people who design, build, and iterate on the service

The long-term value of an engagement is rooted in building the right product team from within the agency. When we look for the people, we look for:

  • Mindset fit — genuine curiosity about the problem, openness to new ways of working, and a collaborative instinct that makes co-creation possible.
  • Readiness to evolve — a willingness to grow with the work, picking up new methods and adapting as technology reshapes what design can do.
  • Domain knowledge — an understanding of the agency’s context, systems, and users that’s hard to bring in from outside.
  • Potential for ownership — the capacity and ambition to eventually lead the work, not just contribute to it.

There are two ways to build this team, and we have a clear preference:

Path 1 — Growing existing talent (preferred)
Path 2 — Bringing in new people

Up-skilling current staff into design, product, or engineering roles retains invaluable domain context. Involving them early means capability builds naturally alongside the engagement. The earlier these conversations start — clearing BAU bandwidth and securing approvals — the smoother the path.

When existing staff can’t be redeployed, fresh talent is an opportunity to build the exact capabilities the engagement needs. To ensure readiness, align early on manpower options — whether navigating MMF allocations, hiring contractors, or securing a longer-term GovTech commitment.

The operations teamThe people who run and sustain it once it’s live

A project’s success ultimately depends on the team running it after launch — the people maintaining, administering, and reviewing performance day to day. Unlike building a traditional product team, new roles may be introduced to fill this operational layer, making structured change management all the more important.

Sustaining this team means working through team structure, funding, hiring, and potential vendor partnerships early. Above all, leadership must clearly define the organisation’s long-term commitment once GTC’s direct involvement ends.

The goal across both dimensions is the same: when we leave, the work continues, our agency partners are confident, and the work improves over time.

This section outlines the full arc of the GTC Design team’s involvement across an engagement’s lifecycle. Our work is structured across four key stages, defining how we partner with agencies from initial alignment to our eventual exit: Qualifying, Discovery, Building & Delivery, and Support & Maintenance.

Diagram — to come

The full engagement arc — a horizontal timeline: Qualifying → Discovery (Subphase 1 + Subphase 2) → Building & Delivering → Support & Maintenance, with Capability Building running as a parallel track underneath.

4.1 Qualifying

Before committing to a collaboration, we conduct a surface-level assessment to ensure an engagement aligns with our strategic focus. Unlike the discovery phase, which is more in-depth, this initial phase is focused on gathering high-level context and establishing a shared purpose.

The process is highly flexible, ranging from a single conversation to a few weeks of light sensing depending on the complexity of the engagement.

Activities

  • Conversations to understand the agency’s asks and requirements.
  • Problem reframing sessions to uncover root challenges.
  • Internal discussions on strategic fit, capacity, and resource shaping.
  • Engagement scoping to establish high-level boundaries around engagement size, timeline, and feasibility.

Artifacts

  • One-pager problem framing and recommendation
  • Engagement brief
  • Project plan

Outcomes

  • A clear decision on commitment; we accept or decline, and can explain why either way.
  • Sharper clarity on the core challenge; the agency leaves understanding it far better than when they walked in.
  • A shared scope, giving both teams one unified picture of what’s in and what’s out.
  • A clearly defined engagement team, with everyone on board and clear on what they own.
  • A directional estimate of effort and timeline, with honest flags on where confidence is still low.

4.2 Discovery

Discovery sits at the heart of our work. It’s an active loop where uncovering insights and testing solutions happen in sequence, but frequently feed back into each other as we learn (more on our philosophy in 2.3: Through a Discovery Lens).

A full discovery journey runs in two subphases, each with a distinct role:

Subphase 1 — Discover to Understand

Frame the problem. Confirm it’s real, systemic, and worth solving before anyone proposes how to build it.

Subphase 2 — Discover to Validate

Test solutions. Hypothesise, prototype, and validate against real user signal until a confident roadmap emerges.

Together, they move an engagement from an ambiguous problem statement to a high-confidence, validated roadmap.

Subphase 1: Discover to UnderstandTo frame a problem

The purpose of Subphase 1 is to define the real problem, and align understanding with our agency partners before a solution is proposed.

The dual-track discovery process

Inside this subphase, discovery runs on two tracks in tandem. What design learns about user needs reshapes the technical questions worth asking; what engineering learns about legacy constraints reshapes what is realistic to explore.

Diagram — to come

The dual-track discovery process — Experience Discovery (design-led) and Technical Discovery (engineer-led) running in tandem, each continuously informing the other.

Experience discovery (design-led)
Technical discovery (engineer-led)

Identify and understand the needs, behaviours, and context of the citizens and officers who will use the service.

Identify what tools already exist, legacy-system constraints, API integration dependencies, and technical risks.

Activities

  • Focus group sessions to gather shared user perspectives.
  • Qualitative interviews to uncover individual insights.
  • Quantitative surveys to validate trends at scale.
  • Field studies to observe real-world user workflows.
  • Usability testing to identify system friction points.
  • Technical infrastructure mapping of pipelines, APIs, and data schemas.

Artifacts

  • Service map
  • Research findings report
  • Shortlist of prioritised use cases
  • Technical discovery report by engineers

Outcomes

  • A shared, objective problem definition; we align on the actual systemic challenge rather than the initially stated request.
  • A comprehensive landscape map; value propositions, user needs, policy implications, and technical constraints are fully surfaced and named.
  • A prioritised use-case roadmap; we lock in a clear, defensible view of what to tackle first, and why, for the next subphase.
  • Clarity on the root issue; the real problem is understood, moving past the surface-level symptoms.

What gets in the way

Every discovery surfaces trade-offs. Policy constraints, siloed mandates, competing agency priorities, and limited resources all create tension. This phase requires naming those trade-offs honestly so leadership can make informed decisions before prototyping begins.

Subphase 2: Discover to ValidateTo rapidly experiment

The purpose of Subphase 2 is to transform the understanding from Subphase 1 into a testable object. We hypothesise solutions, then build dynamic prototypes to get real users testing. The prototypes are continuously refined until a validated solution emerges.

The rapid experimental cycle

This subphase is a repeating loop designed to test hypotheses quickly and cheaply. Each loop runs on a two-to-three-week cadence, with the first user-tested prototype available within three weeks.

The loop: Hypothesis → Quick build → User test → (repeat)

Hypothesis

Start with a clear bet based on Subphase 1 data (e.g. “based on X constraint, we believe Y workflow will reduce processing time”). It must be a concrete proposition, not a vague direction.

Build (quickly)

Turn the hypothesis into a functional prototype that works with real or synthetic data in a realistic workflow.

Test

Put it in front of end-users (citizens or officers). Observe behaviours, collect hard data, and feed the learnings back into the next hypothesis loop.

Core characteristics of Subphase 2

What we run

  • Rapid iteration over scalability. Prototypes are built to learn, not to ship. Teams work in close collaboration, pooling specialised knowledge to generate prototypes that get progressively closer to the solution. The final build that ships after the rapid experimental cycle may look different from these iterations.
  • Real validation. True insight comes from testing with the people who will actually use the thing. Internal reviews and stakeholder walkthroughs are a useful start, but they are not validation — real validation requires observing real users interacting with the system in real contexts. Two rules follow: every prototype needs a clear hypothesis it is testing, and a clear way to measure whether it worked. Without both, a test produces opinions rather than signal.

What we build

  • Functional prototypes. We build functional prototypes that behave like real systems rather than relying on static mockups or click-throughs to validate core logic. Grounded in data insights from earlier discovery, these artifacts integrate directly into real workflows to generate high-signal, actionable validation.

The handoff

Subphase 2 ends with a full handover and, often, our exit from the engagement. We leave behind a validated prototype, real-world workflow mappings, documented decisions, and a roadmap for implementation. From there, the partner’s development team builds the production-ready solution.

Activities

  • Hypothesis shaping based on findings and insights from Subphase 1.
  • Rapid prototyping to deliver 1–3 concepts per engagement within 2–4 weeks each.
  • User testing with real citizens, officers, and relevant stakeholders.
  • Iterating rapid experiment rounds to learn, refine, and improve solutions.
  • Workflow mapping of the operational processes surrounding each validated prototype.

Artifacts

  • Working prototype code
  • Key decisions and lessons learned, including what failed and why
  • Validated learning reports, including data analytics dashboards
  • High-confidence roadmap

Outcomes

  • Rigorous assumption testing; hypotheses are actively proven against reality.
  • A high-confidence roadmap; our next steps rest securely on proven data.
  • Early validation of what works; the team knows exactly what succeeds, and what fails, before committing resources to build.
  • Co-designed solutions; both officer and citizen perspectives have directly shaped the features and concepts taken forward.
  • An adaptable use-case backlog; priorities remain flexible to expand or shift as continuous experimentation reveals new insights.

Navigating prototyping constraints

Building functional prototypes often surfaces roadblocks. Rather than dropping back to static mockups, we’ve used the following strategies to navigate constraints across data, access, and team dynamics:

Data

Building with synthetic data when live databases are unavailable, modelled closely on the real production schema. This lets us test core functionality and system logic while real-data access is being cleared.

Access

Using mocked APIs and isolated environments, even when met with security classifications, missing APIs, or incomplete pipelines.

Team dynamics

Pairing designers with creative technologists to close the gap between design intent and working code. Not every designer needs to code, but the team together has to carry the skills to build something functional.

4.3 Building & Delivery

Once discovery has set a direction that we and our agency partners are confident in, the work transitions into building. This stage is engineering-led: the partner’s developers take the validated prototype and build it to production. Our main role is to help validate the build, ensuring the final product stays true to what’s been tested before it ships to serve the public.

A note on this stage

As the GTC Design team has yet to reach this stage in our active engagements, the following notes are based on our collective external experience. We expect to refine these steps as we execute them alongside our agency partners.

Anticipated process

  • Engineering sprints drive continuous build and delivery cycles to push software to production.
  • Designers sync directly inside active sprints, working concurrently with engineering rather than ahead or behind them.
  • User testing occurs continuously as new features ship, gathering real-world validation from citizens.
  • Design QA ensures production code remains true to the validated user experience.
  • Discovery loops run in parallel, feeding fresh insights back into the upcoming product roadmap.
  • Capability building continues dynamically, growing the partner agency’s confidence and independence throughout the build.

Anticipated handover

  • Shipped product increments
  • An updated roadmap
  • Documentation and release notes

4.4 Support & Maintenance

This stage typically marks our exit. By this point, the agency partner should have the capability to run the product independently (see 3.3: Building Lasting Capability). If an engagement requires us to stay on, our role shifts to helping the partner find their footing and continue building capacity. Our ultimate goal is never to manage the product on their behalf, but to ensure a sustainable handover.

A note on this stage

As the GTC Design team has yet to reach this stage in our active engagements, the following notes are based on our collective external experience. We expect to refine these steps as we execute them alongside our agency partners.

Anticipated process

  • GTC support decreases gradually as the partner agency team finds its footing.
  • Live metrics alert the team to operational issues and usage anomalies needing attention.
  • Team offboarding ensures a deliberate handover, transferring full ownership to the agency rather than defaulting to us.
