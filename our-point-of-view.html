2.1 Foundations that Guide Us

Government services exist to serve everyone. Whether we are designing for a resident accessing a public portal or the officer working the system from inside, our ultimate goal remains the same: ensuring a reliable, effective experience for whoever is on the other side of the screen.

In order to do our work well, we must operate within the guardrails of the public sector.

The system we work in:

  • Public frameworks — that govern what services or products can be built.
  • Governance structure — that shapes how policy decisions are made.
  • Accountability to the public — that serves as the ultimate purpose of our designs.

The breadth we design for:

Equitable access to public services

We design for everyone, including all abilities, age, language, and digital fluency.

A united government experience

We design for the integrity of the entire civic journey, connecting public services into a seamless experience that feels like one government, not many.

Improved services for public officers

We design to improve tools and processes for officers so they can deliver services with greater confidence.

2.2 Our Principles of Execution

Great public services are the result of well-informed and deliberate choices. In a landscape where technical capacity can outpace decision-making, we hold ourselves to the following principles to ensure our work maintains technical rigour and depth, whilst centring human experiences.

Our operating principles

  • Anchoring in discovery. Uncovering what is true at the start ensures we confidently solve the right problem from day one. While the depth of discovery scales to match the project, embedding this practice into our workflow remains non-negotiable. (See 2.3 and 4.2: Discovery.)
  • Validating to understand. Bringing prototypes into early discovery generates real-world signals faster. Because the cost of building has collapsed, using prototypes early serves as our most effective tool for de-risking critical decisions before we scale.
  • Designing for the whole system. Looking beyond the digital interface allows us to shape the entire delivery chain, including policy, process, operations, and public experience. A product only truly succeeds when the ecosystem supporting it thrives. (See 2.3: Through a Discovery Lens.)
  • Building partner capability. Equipping agency partners to own and iterate on the work ensures lasting impact long after we leave. True success means moving beyond temporary delivery to leave our partners entirely self-sufficient. (See 3.3: Building Lasting Capability.)
  • Unifying team effort. Collapsing the boundaries between team members and their specialised disciplines allows us to cross-examine ideas from multiple angles simultaneously. Working as a unified team during discovery lets us navigate new technologies seamlessly, replacing slow handoffs.

What we’re still working out

Where exactly these discipline boundaries should sit. There is no settled answer yet, and we aren’t waiting for one. We are intentionally experimenting our way toward it, and we expect what we learn to evolve how we design and build.

The human anchor in AI

AI has shifted the way we work — from the shape of our team to the velocity of our tools and the methods we use to solve problems. The GTC Design team uses AI to augment three key areas:

  • Our processes: we use AI to scale our operations effectively, freeing up capacity so we can maintain solid internal systems, personalise onboarding, and stay cohesive as we grow.
  • Our craft: we use AI to expand our technical boundaries by building with repeatable tools, allowing us to do more, move faster, and achieve what was previously out of reach.
  • Our output: we use AI for rapid learning through rapid prototyping. Getting real prototypes into users’ hands lets us test assumptions and iterate alongside business teams in real time.

Even so, AI outputs often miss the operational nuances that only exist in the physical world. Public services are deeply human ecosystems, and the human judgment required to navigate them can’t be resolved in a prompt. This judgment is what drives our most important decisions:

  • Mapping out how a new digital tool will change the rhythm of an officer’s daily routines.
  • Translating lived user experiences into long-term design strategies.
  • Turning competing institutional priorities into a roadmap that agencies can act on.

We rely on technology to handle the heavy lifting, but strategy, ethics, and human discernment cannot be replicated by a model. They remain at the core of our craft, and they are the reason our team shows up the way we do.

2.3 Through a Discovery Lens

No matter how complex the legacy structures, or how siloed the agencies may be, finding an engagement’s core problem begins with discovery. When we work with agency partners, our first goal is to build a shared understanding of the systems, constraints, and the people involved, while actively testing early assumptions. As the stated problem is rarely the root problem, racing ahead on assumptions is a fast track to solving a problem that may not exist.

The cost of bypassing discovery

Skipping the discovery phase fundamentally shifts the product’s risk, scope, and strategic focus. Here is what happens when an insight-led endeavour is replaced by unverified assumptions:

Without discovery (assumption-led)
With discovery (insight-led)

High risk: building unverified features that fail post-launch, draining agency time and budget.

Bloated scope: reacting to symptoms by trying to solve every adjacent issue at once.

Output-focused: delivering a rigid product that performs a function but misses the human context.

De-risked: validating concepts early to save effort, cost, and structural friction.

Strategic scope: focusing collective effort on features that drive the highest systemic impact.

Outcome-focused: delivering an adaptable capability that solves a real, verified user problem.

The GTC design path

Complex products and engagements tend to stall in one of two ways.

  • Building without evidence (the blind sprint): building straight into engineering on unverified assumptions, then discovering post-launch that the problem was misunderstood.
  • Discovering without building (the analysis paralysis): months of research that surfaces brilliant insights, but they stay trapped in slide decks and never become working software.

The GTC path runs directly through the middle. We treat discovery and rapid prototyping as one continuous loop, ensuring that every solution we build is grounded in real-world evidence, and every insight we uncover is immediately tested in a working prototype.

What discovery means to us: a two-phase approach

The landscape of discovery is changing as quickly as the technology around it, but our purpose remains unchanged: to find what’s true before building a solution. What has changed is our ability to now get from insight to evidence faster than ever.

Rather than treating discovery as a linear phase, the GTC Design team sees discovery as a loop that runs in two subphases which then feed each other: (1) Discover What’s True and (2) Discover What Works.

Subphase 1: Discover What’s True(To frame a problem)

To anchor our strategy in facts rather than assumptions, we continue to analyse the current landscape. Mapping these systemic realities and stakeholder constraints aligns agency partners around the root problem, ensuring resources aren’t wasted. (See 4.2: Discovery — Subphase 1.)

Subphase 2: Discover What Works(To rapidly experiment)

To conduct iterative solutioning that catches operational friction early, we pull prototyping out of late-stage development and into early discovery, ensuring ideas meet reality before we commit engineering effort. Testing these prototypes against the data validates our direction with concrete evidence and captures actual user behaviour before development begins. This ultimately saves officers from administrative roadblocks and delivers a reliable experience for the public. (See 4.2: Discovery — Subphase 2.)

2.4 Success in our eyes

The pace of development has accelerated dramatically. But speed applied to the wrong problem is just the wrong problem arrived at sooner. As public needs continue to evolve over time, getting it right takes more than moving quickly; it requires the judgment to know when to push forward and when to slow down — especially within institutions where the default reaction is to settle for “how it’s always been done”.

Below are the indicators that show we are moving in the right direction:

  • The right problems were named. We identified and articulated the real, discovered problem, rather than settling for what was assumed.
  • The hard line was held. We pushed back on assumptions and legacy framings rather than accepting them at face value, to protect the integrity of the final product.
  • The technology served the people. We empowered partner agencies to deploy user-centric solutions that accurately solved real friction points and elevated the citizen experience.
  • The agency’s capabilities were built. We established sustainable operational frameworks, enabling agency partners to drive the work forward without long-term dependency on GTC.
  • The standard was modelled. We demonstrated a level of rigour, empathy, and speed that set a new operational template for the agency’s future endeavours.
